# Module 1 — What is AI and why does it matter?

**Duration:** 90 minutes (adaptable to 60 or 120 minutes)
**Format:** Facilitated group session or self-paced
**Level:** No prior technical knowledge required

---

## Learning outcomes

By the end of this module, learners will be able to:

1. Describe what AI systems are in plain language, without technical jargon
2. Identify at least three AI systems they already interact with in daily life
3. Distinguish between what AI can do well and where it commonly fails
4. Explain why understanding AI matters for their own context

---

## Opening scenario (10 minutes)

> Amina is applying for a job online. She submits her CV and receives an automated rejection within 30 seconds — before any human could have read it. Her friend tells her the company uses an AI screening system. Amina wonders: what did the AI see? What did it miss? Can she appeal?

**Discussion prompt:** Has anything like this happened to you or someone you know? What questions did it raise?

*Facilitator note: This scenario is designed to create immediate personal relevance. Let learners share briefly before moving on — 3 to 4 minutes is enough. The goal is to establish that AI decisions already affect people's real lives, not to resolve the scenario.*

---

## Core content

### What AI actually is (20 minutes)

AI stands for Artificial Intelligence. In practice, when people say "AI" today they usually mean **machine learning systems** — software that has been trained on large amounts of data to recognise patterns and make predictions or decisions.

Key points to cover:

- AI is not a single technology. It is a category that includes many different tools and approaches.
- Most AI systems are trained on examples — they learn from data, not from rules written by hand.
- AI systems produce outputs: a classification, a recommendation, a generated piece of text or an image. These outputs are statistical predictions, not facts or decisions made by a thinking mind.
- AI does not understand the world. It recognises patterns in data.

**Grounding activity:** Ask learners to name things they have used today that might involve AI. Build a list on the board or shared document. Common examples: search engine results, social media feeds, navigation apps, spam filters, autocomplete on phones.

---

### What AI can and cannot do (25 minutes)

**AI is often good at:**
- Pattern recognition in large datasets (spotting unusual transactions in banking data)
- Generating text or images that look plausible (not always accurate)
- Translation between languages (with errors)
- Recommending content based on past behaviour
- Answering common questions quickly

**AI commonly struggles with:**
- Understanding context, nuance, cultural meaning
- Situations that differ from the data it was trained on
- Knowing when it does not know something
- Fairness — AI trained on biased data produces biased outputs
- Explaining its own reasoning in a way humans can verify

**Case discussion:** Return to Amina's scenario. What might the AI screening system have been trained to look for? What kind of CVs might it have been trained on? What might it miss?

---

### Why this matters (15 minutes)

AI systems are making or influencing decisions in:
- Hiring and recruitment
- Credit and loan approvals
- Healthcare triage and diagnosis support
- Criminal justice (bail, sentencing recommendations in some countries)
- Education (grading, admissions)
- Content moderation

The people most affected by AI decisions are not always the people who build or buy those systems. Understanding how AI works — even at a basic level — is part of being informed in a digital society.

---

## Application activity (15 minutes)

**Individual or pair task:**

Think of one decision that affects your life or work that might involve an AI system — or that you think should never be made by an AI system. Write two to three sentences explaining:

1. What the decision is
2. Why AI might or might not be appropriate for it
3. What you would want a human to check or decide

Share with the group if comfortable.

## Facilitator notes

- This module works best when learners can connect the content to their own context. Encourage local examples.
- If learners have strong opinions about AI (very positive or very negative), acknowledge these and invite critical thinking rather than dismissing or reinforcing them.
- The Amina scenario can be replaced with a context more relevant to your learner group — healthcare, agriculture, education or government services all have relevant AI applications.
- Timing is flexible. The opening scenario and core content are the essential elements. The application activity can be shortened or set as homework.
